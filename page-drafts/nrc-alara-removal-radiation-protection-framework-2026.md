# NRC Proposes Removing ALARA From Radiation Protection Rules — What Licensees Need to Know

**Direct answer:** The NRC has proposed eliminating the "as low as reasonably achievable" (ALARA) principle as a standalone regulatory requirement from its radiation protection regulations. Existing numerical dose limits for workers and the public are **not changing**. Instead, the NRC would replace ALARA with a graded, risk-informed approach to dose management that focuses on compliance with established dose limits. The proposed rule (Docket NRC-2025-1140) was published July 15, 2026 in the Federal Register, with a public comment deadline of **August 31, 2026**.

## What Is Changing

For more than 50 years, NRC regulations have required licensees to keep radiation exposures "as low as reasonably achievable" — a principle known as ALARA. The proposed rule would:

- **Remove ALARA** as a standalone regulatory requirement from 10 CFR Parts 19, 20, 34, 35, 40, 50, 61, 71, 72, and 150.
- **Replace ALARA** with a "graded approach to dose management" — as doses increase toward established limits, increasingly more rigorous radiation protection measures are required.
- **Retain all existing numerical dose limits**, including the 5 rem/year occupational whole-body effective dose limit and 100 mrem/year public dose limit.
- **Expand flexibility** for licensees to use modern dose assessment methodologies.
- **Expand occupational dose management options**, including a new dose limit extension process allowing a worker to receive up to twice the annual dose limit in a given year if sufficient "retrospective dose" from prior years is available.
- **Revise caregiver dose limits** under Part 20: a 2-rem limit per administration regimen for caregivers of radiation therapy patients, and a 500-mrem limit for non-caregiver members of the public per regimen.

---

## What Is NOT Changing

- The 5 rem/year (50 mSv/year) occupational whole-body effective dose limit.
- The 100 mrem/year (1 mSv/year) dose limit for members of the public.
- Declared pregnant worker dose limits.
- Dose limits for minors.
- Prior licensing conditions that included ALARA commitments — these remain in effect due to their "inherently conservative nature."
- The requirement to comply with all applicable dose limits.

---

## Why the NRC Is Making This Change

The proposed rule is driven by Executive Order 14300 (May 2025), which directed the NRC to reconsider the ALARA principle and the linear no-threshold (LNT) model for radiation exposure. The NRC concluded that:

> Retaining ALARA as a separate regulatory expectation can lead to additional costs and complexity without a measurable safety benefit.

The agency found that ALARA's open-ended requirement for continuous dose reduction — without a defined stopping point — can make compliance burdensome and subjective, without producing proportionate safety benefits at low dose levels.

---

## What the Graded Approach Means in Practice

Under the proposed framework, radiation protection requirements scale with how close doses are to established limits:

| Dose Level | Expected Protection Measures |
|---|---|
| Well below limits (low zone) | Basic compliance measures; no additional optimization required |
| Moderate zone | Routine engineering and administrative controls |
| Approaching limits (elevated zone) | Increasingly rigorous controls, work planning, shielding reviews |
| At or near limit | Mandatory enhanced measures; dose limit not to be exceeded |

The NRC has developed guidance on an "acceptable means" for implementing this graded approach, including specific radiation protection measures corresponding to each threshold.

---

## Impact on Radiation Safety Programs

For most NRC and Agreement State licensees currently operating well below their dose limits, the NRC expects **existing practices will already comply** with the updated rule. However, licensees should review their radiation safety programs for:

1. **Written ALARA program policies** that reference the ALARA standard by name — these will need to be updated to reference the graded approach.
2. **License conditions with ALARA commitments** — these remain in effect; coordinate with your RSO before removing any documentation.
3. **ALARA training content** — worker training programs under 10 CFR 19 and 10 CFR 35 may need to be revised.
4. **Annual ALARA reports and optimization reviews** — these may no longer be required once the rule is final, but Agreement State licensees should await state adoption before making changes.
5. **Caregiver release instructions** under Part 35 — if your facility releases patients after therapeutic administrations, updated release criteria may be available under the new caregiver dose limits.

## Agreement State Licensees

New England states (Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Vermont, New Hampshire, and Maine) are all NRC Agreement States. Agreement State licensees are regulated by their state radiation control program, not directly by the NRC. Agreement States must adopt compatible regulations within 3 years of NRC final rule publication.

This means the final rule (expected November 2026) would not automatically apply to Agreement State licensees until their state formally adopts the changes. We will track and publish state adoption timelines here as they become available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does removing ALARA mean my facility can let doses increase?**
No. All existing numerical dose limits remain unchanged. The proposed rule removes ALARA as a separate optimization obligation but requires compliance with all established dose limits and mandates more rigorous controls as doses approach those limits.

**Will my current radiation safety program need to be rewritten?**
For most licensees, the NRC expects existing practices already comply with the new framework. However, written policies, ALARA training, and annual ALARA reports will likely need to be updated once the rule is finalized.

**When does this take effect?**
The final rule is targeted for November 2026. Agreement State licensees must await state adoption before the rule applies to them.

**What is a "graded approach to dose management"?**
A graded approach means that radiation protection requirements increase in stringency as occupational doses approach established limits. It replaces ALARA's open-ended optimization requirement with a more objective, threshold-based framework.

**Does this affect medical use licensees (Part 35)?**
Yes. In addition to Part 20 changes, the proposal introduces new caregiver dose limits for radiation therapy patients (2 rem per administration regimen for caregivers; 500 mrem per regimen for non-caregivers) that work alongside existing patient release regulations.

**Where can I submit public comments?**
Comments are accepted at regulations.gov using Docket ID NRC-2025-1140, through August 31, 2026.
